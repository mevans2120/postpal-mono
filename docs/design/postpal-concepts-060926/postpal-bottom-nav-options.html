Option 3: the bar answers "what's next" forever. The left slot holds whatever is genuinely next in her recovery — dose (clay, urgent), milestone or cycle check-in (pine, calm), the MRI (ink, formal). The color shift quietly communicates register without new layouts. "Feeling something?" stays constant and quiet on the right. → Survives all four phases with one component. The crops are the argument.
Option 3's risk: a smart slot needs smart rules. "Next" must pick one thing — dose vs milestone vs appointment — and a wrong pick erodes trust. The priority logic (dose > red-flag window > appointment > milestone > cycle) is real product work hiding in a small pill. → Prototype question: does she understand why "Next" changed?
Option 4: discoverable, familiar, testable. Four labeled tabs — Today, Medicines (dose badge when due), Check, More. Nothing has to be inferred; everything has a place. The palette and icon weight keep it from reading fully clinical. → Best for first-session orientation and for older or less app-fluent patients.
Option 4's cost: chrome that outlives its content. By week 7 the Medicines tab is a dead limb (shown dimmed in the crop) and "Today" still implies daily duty in the once-a-cycle phase. Tab bars are static by nature; this product's needs aren't. → Also the register risk: labeled tabs are the first genuinely portal-like element in the concept.
Expanded Next: a day rail, not a calendar. The sheet is a timeline in the Daybook's grammar — grouped by this-morning / next / later-today rather than hourly slots. Done doses dim and strike through like receipts; only the next dose carries color and a Log button; PRN meds wait in their own group with availability stated in words ("ok again now"). The guardrail meter, quiet line, and support pairing close the sheet — the salvaged Night Stand engine, rehoused. → Answers "is it a timeline?": yes, but a rail of moments, not a grid of hours. One decision is loud; everything else is reference.
"Expanded" follows the slot's meaning across phases. Next always opens the thing it names: the med rail in the acute phase, the can/can't sheet at day 9, the one-question cycle check-in (faces again) at week 7, the MRI appointment page at month 3. One interaction contract, four sheet shapes. → The slot and its sheet age together — no dead chrome at any phase.
How to decide. If the prototype test shows patients hunting for things (lostness), Option 4 wins on orientation. If they find the flow naturally, Option 3 keeps the product's voice and ages with the recovery. A hybrid also exists: Option 3's pair during the acute phase, graduating to nothing but the status line after week 2. → Recommend testing both in the interactive prototype with one day-3 patient and one week-7 scenario.
